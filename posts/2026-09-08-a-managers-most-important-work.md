# A Manager's Most Important Work

_Do managers make a difference to their teams?_

_What are the most important things a manager can do to make their teams more effective?_

Research from Google tells us yes: create a team environment that supports and grows everyone.

Today I am going to focus on the behaviors that the research says have the biggest impact. And it may surprise you to see how many of these are day-to-day habits and practices that managers can start prioritizing for themselves today.

I once coached an engineer who was considering moving from individual contributor to manager, but had a bad experience when he had tried management earlier. When we discussed that previous experience, it became clear he had come into that experience expecting it to continue emphasizing the same areas that had led him to be an excellent engineer: being the most technical person in the room, making all the decisions, demonstrating ownership and accountability through presence. It is a common pattern for new managers, and one that fails them and their teams. Manager is a different job from individual contributor and the research shows the top drivers of success aren't what most individual contributors expect.

## Be a Good Coach

This was the #1 trait of effective managers in the research. What makes for a good coach? Providing consistent regular feedback, asking questions instead of dictating orders, and actively supporting professional growth. This may show up as a manager asking questions in a design doc on mitigating a potential technical risk instead of telling them the solution to implement. Or privately debriefing with a report about how a meeting with a partner team went and strategizing together on approaches for the future. Or ensuring 1:1s focus on individual growth & development instead of project status. A coach does not fix problems, but builds day-to-day problem solving and core skills in their reports.

AI can be an incredible tool to aid managers in improving their coaching, flagging important moments for lightweight feedback that might otherwise fly by. However, a key consideration in coaching is attentional focus and AI doesn't seem able to consistently provide that yet.

- How do you rate yourself as a coach?
- Do you deliver challenging feedback in a timely and constructive manner?
- Is your feedback specific and directly actionable?

## Empower the Team Without Micromanaging

Micromanagement is a leading cause of employee burnout and low innovation, and Google found that the opposite behavior in empowering teams was a strong predictor of high performance.

Empowerment looks like:

- Defining the goal, but leaving the how to the engineer.
- Acting as a safety net & expeditor.
- Rewarding success as measured by outcomes and impacts instead of process or presence.
- Using "stretch" assignments to accelerate growth with support.
- Letting engineers own decisions to do things differently than the manager would have.
- Treating mistakes and failures as learning opportunities.

Empowering managers foster personal responsibility and accountability within their teams, leading to deep engagement and improved problem solving. The manager's own time and technical capabilities no longer act as a cap on what the team can do.

This does not mean abandoning engineers to sink or swim. It means giving them more freedom and accountability than feels comfortable. It means treating that discomfort as part of your job, not a warning sign.

This is an area where the AI rhetoric is quite strong. Who needs managers? However, I think the story often misses the need for that accountability to be individually calibrated. I've also heard approaches that swing the completely opposite direction with founders or SVPs directly assigning tiny units of work to engineers without the context or freedom to take different approaches to the underlying problem. Neither of these align with what the research shows to be a successful strategy for engineer empowerment.

## Create an Inclusive Team Environment

Inclusivity for managers is about building a team climate where every employee feels valued, respected, and supported, regardless of their background. In later research this was tied to providing a foundation for "psychological safety".

What did this research mean by "inclusivity"?

1. Fostering "speaking-up cultures" where everyone participates, not just a few dominant voices.
2. Treating employees as whole people instead of resources: respecting personal boundaries, managing workloads to prevent burnout, and celebrating personal milestones outside of work.
3. Providing transparency, especially around tough decisions, and listening deeply.
4. Ensuring that high-visibility, career-advancing projects are distributed fairly.

In essence, treating people as humans instead of cogs, and creating a sense of fairness and transparency within the team.

AI tools can be excellent at flagging when someone isn't getting a fair chance to participate, and even suggesting ways to improve it. However, it's on the manager to address problems when they occur, proactively drive fairness, make space for all voices, and treat everyone on the team as human.

## It Comes Down to Psychological Safety

If you wondered where the phrase "psychological safety" came up in the context of work, Project Aristotle research was definitely part of it. It is about creating the space where team members can take risks in front of the group, stretch their own skills, bring in fresh ideas, and speak up when something isn't working. And it is the foundation at Google for building effective teams.

Psychological safety looks like:

- Teams supporting each other instead of blaming when problems arise.
- Everyone feels comfortable asking for help and admitting when they don't know something.
- Everyone on the team contributes to discussions.

While the entire team is part of psychological safety, a manager plays an outsized role in building and supporting that culture for a team.

In her TEDx talk, Dr Amy Edmondson of Harvard offers three simple things individuals can do to foster team psychological safety:

1. Frame the work as a learning problem, not an execution problem.
2. Acknowledge your own fallibility.
3. Model curiosity and ask lots of questions.

Here AI can be a tool for monitoring and diagnosis of problems, but can't model behavior itself.

## Supports Career Development

I wanted to briefly talk about a related item in the list because of how closely it relates to the others: career development. These conversations are critical tools for a manager to build psychological safety for their team. The key is to not just evaluate past behavior, but to actively invest in individual growth.

- Provide positive and constructive feedback routinely, not just at annual review.
- Use a framework like SBI to ground feedback and ensure it is specific and actionable.
- Support employees in figuring out solutions to their career goals rather than dictating.

And that is what I did with the engineer I mentioned at the start of the article. Naming those behaviors clearly helped him recontextualize his past experience. The problem was that he was still running the individual-performance playbook in a role that required driving team performance. In particular, overemphasizing old individual contributor strengths as a manager can undermine team empowerment.
