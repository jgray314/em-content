# Getting Things Done

If the most critical behaviors of great managers are about building the psychological safety to allow for teams to take good risks, the next most important set of behaviors are all about ensuring the team can get things done.

## Focus on Productivity and Results

How do top managers measure their teams?
Hours in the office? Lines of code shipped? Absolutely not!

The best managers set the right goals for the team in terms of outcomes, give individuals autonomy to solve problems, and watch for when the team is meeting the metric instead of the underlying objective. If someone realizes two weeks in that a proposed project is fundamentally flawed and won't work and they share it? The best managers celebrate it. Find a way to achieve the outcome in 2 days instead of 2 months? Awesome, even if code is no longer needed.

More than that, effective managers pitch in to help clear blocks, remove hurdles, and maintain focus on what matters most. Team members may tap a manager to mediate when a design review process gets stuck, or to expedite a blocking request to another team, or to explain to partners why a requested feature will be added to a backlog instead of addressed immediately. These are about ensuring the team has focus and can keep making progress. Just as important is that a manager goes out of their way to not be the blocker themselves.

This is another space where AI tooling can greatly assist, say by encouraging the use of frameworks like SMART goals and paired results, but it will not apply itself.

## Communicate Effectively

When it comes to effective communication for teams getting things done there are two things managers must do:

1. Listen to their team
2. Share information openly

Listening is about supporting communication out from individual contributors. It is about the manager being able to understand what is on their team's mind and being able to address it or clearly share it as appropriate. It is about surfacing and consolidating the knowledge of the group as a whole to guide efforts. A manager often acts as a representative for their team, and to do that job well, they must understand the team.

Sharing information openly is about ensuring critical information is coming in. Ensuring every member of the team understands the team goals, plans, and strategies. That individuals understand the whys behind decisions so they can appropriately adapt execution on the fly. That the people doing the work know about changes that impact the project and associated company level goals.

AI can be a fantastic facilitator here, but typically lacks the context and judgement to connect all relevant news to a team's mission. It certainly can't understand on behalf of others, and has limited capacity to evaluate the understanding generally. It also has an unfortunate tendency towards content overload.

## Implement a Clear Vision and Strategy

It is hard to reach a destination as quickly as possible without a map. Vision and strategy act as that map for a team, setting the goal and providing a space for everyone on the team to contribute in figuring out the best path to achieve that goal. The strongest managers provide updates on the wider context so everyone understands the big picture, and connect everyday tasks back to the strategy so individuals understand why their work matters.

These are all critical to empower everyone to come up with the best path possible to achieve goals. Work without a connection to outcomes is a path to burnout and disengagement.

My own organization has at times struggled with this. We had a major organizational change where engineering started reporting to an org separate from our close cross functional partners, with each of those overarching orgs having very different scopes and stated overarching goals. With some effort we were able to identify a core set of ownership and goals for the cross functional team that met both orgs most critical needs as the wider vision alignment was worked out.

You may wonder what vision looks like for an anti-fraud team, assuming it is reactive and tactical to the activities of bad actors. If anything, I found having a vision and strategy was even more critical for success. We needed to embed the company values into our system approach and invest in mitigating classes of attack rather than specific observed patterns to stay ahead as volume grew. It was only with a clear vision we saw strong persistent results instead of a chaotic layering of patches.

There are a number of areas where AI can assist with vision and strategy: facilitating the ideation process and reviewing OKRs against best practice frameworks to aid in refinement are some obvious options. It can't embed a vision as a central part of a team's thinking and decision-making yet.
