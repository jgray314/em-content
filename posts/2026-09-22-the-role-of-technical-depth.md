# The Role of Technical Depth

You may be wondering where domain expertise and technical depth fit into the research. They fit in, but are far less critical for manager and team success than building the right team environment and getting things done.

## Have Key Technical Skills

What do technical skills look like? Project Oxygen wasn't just looking at engineers.

- An engineering manager who has built critical infrastructure for multiple products.
- A risk policy head with years of risk operations and analysis experience.
- A head of design with over a decade of execution work across multiple companies.

What ties these examples together is that in each case, the leader knows enough to

- Give advice to help the team solve hard problems.
- Grasp the daily tasks of the group.
- Build trust in their decisions and advice.

It is not uncommon for new managers who were exceptionally strong ICs to come in thinking they should lean into purely technical and execution skills. However the role of those skills has changed. Instead of directly executing those skills, excellent managers use them to build credibility with the team and partners. For all but the smallest teams, a manager should focus on advising and growing instead of doing things themselves.

A manager should be a force multiplier for their whole team. Knowledge and experience aid in that, but can make the manager into a bottleneck if they try to do everything for themselves. Their role is to upskill their team so everyone on it can have a wider impact.

One of the things I appreciate most in my career is that I repeatedly (Google, Tune, Sift) moved to opportunities where I would be in a technical lead or management role with a clearly more technically deep individual contributor as a critical partner. I needed to be able to deeply engage with the problems they were solving, but I had clear responsibilities that did not and should not have depended on me being the most technical person in the room. Working together we were able to lead teams in ways we couldn't have alone.

This is an area where AI can have a strong impact, in particular, checking work against and helping teams consistently follow best practices. AI can be a key tool around a manager's application of their technical skills to their team.

## Collaborate Across the Company

What does great collaboration look like? When a major fraud attack was in progress the risk organization would set up regular syncs with representation across engineering, data, policy, and ops to ensure aligned goals and priority. When an attack was targeted at a particular product or end point, we'd bring in product and engineering partners from those teams so they could understand the impact and take part in solutioning. Collaboration allowed us to move faster and with more confidence.

Great managers share insights across teams and groups, break down silos, and actively seek opportunities for partnership. They understand not only the company and organizational vision for their team, but reach across those boundaries to build better and faster. They can identify duplicated efforts or opportunities where working together would lead to better outcomes for the company as a whole, and work to execute on those opportunities.

Again AI is a tool that can aid with these efforts, but will not do the work for a manager or team on its own.

## Make Strong Decisions

This one is easy to say, but not always clear in what it means. It means making timely choices that align with team goals, even when information is missing. This means launching before you have full certainty, and using real-world data to inform the next decision. It means tie breaking between two good options to keep things moving forward. It means focusing a team's efforts so the most important work is being done.

Great managers make decisions that are:

- Timely
- Well-informed
- Aligned with team & company objectives
- Intentional with trade-offs
- Important and tough

This was a common need in my Risk organization. I and the wider org could not know how fraudsters would interact with a new product launch. However we could use our experience and judgement to put in place appropriate monitoring, safety guardrails, and break glass controls to support a launch with confidence in spite of the unknowns.

Of these, AI is best placed to be a tool for ensuring decisions are well-informed and intentionally consider tradeoffs. However it can not provide timeliness or accountability for decisions or a lack of them.

In the end these domain skills are still important, but the research makes clear they are not as critical for manager and team success as people and team execution skills.
