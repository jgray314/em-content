# Research Driven Management

In 2026, several companies are experimenting with replacing managers with "player-coaches" and AI tooling. It's not the first time someone's tried this.

In 2002, Google tried a radical experiment: getting rid of all engineering managers. Too many people went directly to founder Larry Page with questions about expense reports, interpersonal conflicts, and other nitty-gritty issues. The experiment of a completely flat organization lasted only a few months, and eventually sparked novel research into what makes for effective management.

While it was gone when I joined Google in 2005, there were still signs of the experiment. My manager when I started was an SVP with over 200 reports.

We never met.

While some folks were able to bridge the gap between university and the working world without this support, I struggled at times. I didn't quite understand the expectations on me and really where I ought to focus my efforts. This was especially true as someone with wide ranging interests and a desire to unblock things even when it wasn't my lane. In hindsight, I put a lot of my time and effort in lower impact areas when an early nudge from a leader would have set me on a better course. Even once I got that managerial support, I already had a suite of bad habits to unlearn before I could truly make progress.

The bulk of my career at Google happened during these years of research. The findings have been critical in how I developed my own management style to facilitate creativity, team success, and positive business outcomes. The first of these was Project Oxygen, a multi-year study with over 10,000 quantitative and qualitative datapoints. The answer to "Do managers matter?" was a resounding yes. This was followed up by Project Aristotle with the key insight that how a team works together is more important than who is on the team. Given I was one of the individual contributors covered by the research, maybe it shouldn't be surprising that the findings resonated with me, and acted as a critical guidepost as I became a tech lead and later engineering manager.

The research answers key questions for any manager:

- What behaviors move teams?
- How do managers get things done?
- Where does a strong background as an individual contributor fit in the manager toolbelt?

This was a big part of why and how I stayed in a tech lead role even as more senior engineers joined. I wasn't the most experienced person in the room, but I and others could see that creating the conditions for others to do great work resulted in a multiplicative impact on the team. And while I made mistakes along the way I was able to learn from my team as well.

Over the next few articles I'll be diving into how the research answers each of these questions and my own experiences around them in 20 years of software and 10 as a manager and engineering leader. The cost of getting this wrong isn't abstract. I've seen talented engineers leave teams not because the work was bad, but because the management was. I've been in environments where technically brilliant managers created environments where no one felt safe enough to flag a problem until it was too late. The research matters because the alternative, managing on instinct, or not managing at all, has a predictable track record.

The research is clear that management is a skill, not a title: a set of specific, observable, and learnable behaviors. That's precisely what makes the "just replace managers" conversation worth taking seriously and worth pushing back on. While AI can absolutely help with the mechanical parts of the job, what the data says moves teams, trust, clarity, coaching, psychological safety, is harder to automate. Over the next four articles, I'll walk through exactly what those behaviors are, drawn from Project Oxygen, and where I think AI genuinely helps versus where it can't substitute for human work.
