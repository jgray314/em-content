# New Manager Traps

I've spent a few articles talking about Project Oxygen and what makes for effective managers, but what should individual contributors transitioning into management keep top of mind? The answer is traps. These are suggested by the research, but they each deserve their own call out.

I once had an individual contributor reporting to me who had a bad experience when they first tried to be a manager and was very hesitant to try again in spite of it being a good match to their strengths and interests. When they were considering giving it another try I created a new manager guide. When I listed common traps they realized they had fallen into three of them with their first attempt. That information recontextualized their prior experience and let them return to a manager role with confidence.

It wasn't a surprise they fell into these traps. I'd seen them in my own managers, and partner teams, and, embarrassingly enough, myself. Knowing they are there is a first step, but no guarantee you'll avoid them.

## Trap 1: Thinking Manager is a Promotion

*I was offered a manager role because I was a great individual contributor. I should lean into those skills.*

A reasonable sounding assumption, but wrong. Becoming a manager is a lateral move into a completely new profession. Success is no longer about personal output, but the impact of your team as a whole.

For all but the smallest of teams your day will change dramatically. Your differentiated contribution will be guiding, coaching, unblocking, and scaling the impact of your team. Functionally your reports are no longer your team, your peers and partners across functions and organizations are.

It is a tough change. You got here by being the fastest and most technical. Those instincts feel like safe answers under pressure, but that is a trap.

## Trap 2: Doing Everything Yourself

*I am now responsible for the team. I should personally make the decisions and check all the work.*

If you are routinely writing code instead of unblocking your team, congratulations, you've turned yourself into the bottleneck. That isn't to say you can't or shouldn't stay close to the technology, but especially as a new manager that needs to be a careful and conscious choice. Your success metric has shifted to your team's results, and you will likely need to play a part in defining and selling what those results should be.

You also should be thinking about how to grow the capabilities with your team, not just architecturally, but through the development of individuals and organizational design. You need to do the taxing work of delegating even when it is a little uncomfortable. Any time you tackle the work for yourself instead of letting someone stretch, you are choosing short term delivery over long term capability for your team. Without that you are setting a low ceiling on yours and your team's potential. Within that learning to coach without micromanaging is an art, and one that requires you to accept accountability without taking control. It is about scaling systems of people.

## Trap 3: Not Changing Your Communication

*I can keep communicating with my reports the same way I did when they were my peers.*

You cannot be someone's manager without it impacting your communication. It is inherent to the changed power dynamic. Pretending otherwise will lead to more misunderstandings and unproductive conflict. You will need to both change how you communicate and invest in your people skills.

Managers need to have the uncomfortable conversations early, before problems can fester and grow unmanageable. They will need to build the muscle of giving direct and timely feedback and do so consistently.

## On Becoming a Manager

Becoming a manager is a challenging transition, but it can be a very fulfilling one if you know what you are signing up for. Hopefully this series has helped you understand what is needed to be a top manager and if that is the right path for you.

I certainly felt challenged but fulfilled on my own path. The same was true for that report. They returned to the manager role and excelled at it.
